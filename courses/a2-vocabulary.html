Part 1 · covers the three roles

The people: data subject, controller, processor 8 min live

Every privacy story has three characters. There is the person the data is about, the organisation that decides why and how the data gets used, and the helper that only acts on that organisation's instructions. Learn these three and you can place anyone in any data arrangement. The names are data subject, controller, and processor - and yes, the PDPA uses different words for two of them, which we note below.

LiveThe data subject - the human behind the row2 min▶

The data subject is simply the person the personal data is about - your customer, your employee, your newsletter reader. It is the term we met at the end of Session 1, and it is the one to keep in your head, because every rule that follows exists to protect this person. When you are unsure whether something is allowed, picture the data subject reading over your shoulder: would they be surprised, or comfortable?

  • Always a living person, never a company. A business is not a data subject.
  • One record can involve many data subjects - a group photo, a shared chat, a list of attendees.
LiveThe controller - the one who decides3 min▶

The controller is the organisation that decides why personal data is collected and how it is used. If you choose to build an email list, and you choose what to do with it, you are the controller of that list. The controller carries the responsibility - it is the one the rules hold accountable. Singapore's PDPA calls this player the "organisation", but the meaning is the same: whoever calls the shots on the data.

  • The test is decision-making power: who sets the purpose and the means?
  • Two organisations can be joint controllers when they decide together.
LiveThe processor - the helper who follows instructions3 min▶

The processor handles personal data on the controller's behalf and only does what it is told. Your payroll vendor, your cloud host, your email-sending tool - they touch the data, but they do not decide the purpose. The PDPA name for this role is "data intermediary". The key line is simple: a processor that starts deciding things for itself has quietly become a controller, and picks up a controller's responsibilities.

Real world

A neighbourhood shop and its email tool. Picture a small shop that emails a monthly promotion. The shop is the controller - it decided to build the list and what to send. The email platform it uses is the processor - it stores the addresses and presses send, but only when the shop tells it to. And the customer is the data subject - the person the addresses belong to. Three roles, one everyday errand.

Self-studyWhy the labels matter for who is on the hook2 min read▶

These are not just tidy words - they decide who is responsible when something goes wrong. The controller owes the duties to the data subject: give notice, respect the purpose, honour rights, keep it safe. The processor owes its duties to the controller: follow the contract, do not go off-script, help when the controller needs to answer a request. When you sign up a new vendor, you are almost always the controller and they are the processor - which means the buck still stops with you. That is why a written agreement setting out what the processor may and may not do is a governance staple, not red tape.

Part 2 · covers the three promises

The promises: consent, purpose, notice 7 min live

If the roles are the cast, the promises are the plot. Almost everything a privacy law asks of a controller boils down to three plain commitments: tell people what you are doing (notice), get their permission for it (consent), and then stick to what you said (purpose limitation). Keep those three and you are most of the way there.

LiveConsent - permission for a stated purpose2 min▶

Consent is permission to use someone's data for a purpose you have told them about. Good consent is a clear yes to a clear ask - not a pre-ticked box, not buried in ten pages of terms. And because it is permission for a stated purpose, it does not stretch: consent to email you a receipt is not consent to add you to a marketing list. Consent is one lawful way to use data - there are others - but it is the one beginners meet first, and the easiest to get wrong by being vague.

  • Clear, specific, and freely given beats broad and buried.
  • People can take it back - see the rights in Part 3.
LivePurpose limitation - stick to what you said2 min▶

Purpose limitation is the promise that turns consent into trust: use the data only for the purpose you collected it for. You gathered emails to send order updates - so you do not suddenly sell them, feed them to a new AI experiment, or blast a promotion, unless you go back and ask again. This one rule prevents most "that felt creepy" moments, because surprise is exactly what it rules out. Its close cousin is data minimisation: collect only what the purpose needs, and keep it only as long as the purpose lasts.

LiveNotice - tell people, in words they understand2 min▶

Notice (some laws say notification) is simply telling people what you collect, why, and what you will do with it - before or as you collect it. It is the privacy notice on your website, the line under a signup form, the short explanation at the till. Good notice is honest and readable; nobody is served by a wall of legalese. Notice is what makes consent meaningful: you cannot truly agree to something you were never told.

Real world

The signup box that got it right. A small studio added one line under its newsletter form: "We will use your email to send our monthly note and nothing else. Unsubscribe anytime." That single sentence is notice (what and why), sets the purpose (monthly note only), and signals a right (withdraw) - three promises in twenty words. Sign-ups went up, because clarity reads as respect.

Self-studyRecap: personal vs sensitive data2 min read▶

Carrying over from Session 1: personal data is anything that can identify one living person, and sensitive data (the GDPR's "special category") is the slice that could hurt most if mishandled - health, race, religion, sexual orientation, political views, biometrics. The promises above apply to all personal data, but sensitive data raises the bar on every one of them: you need a stronger reason to collect it, tighter protection while you hold it, and more care before you share it. When you meet the vocabulary in the wild, watch for phrases like "we may use your health information" - that is a signal the highest bar should be in play.

Part 3 · covers the rights

The rights, in one breath 5 min live

The flip side of a controller's promises is a set of rights the data subject can exercise. Say them in one breath: people can be informed, access a copy, correct errors, and withdraw consent. The GDPR adds two more you will hear about: erasure (the "right to be forgotten") and portability (getting your data in a portable form). Singapore's PDPA gives the first four in spirit; the GDPR reaches further.

LiveThe rights, plainly - and where GDPR goes further3 min▶

You do not need the article numbers. You need to recognise these when a person asks, and know that a working organisation can answer:

  • Be informed - know what you hold about them and why (this is notice, from their side).
  • Access - get a copy of the personal data you hold about them.
  • Correct - fix data that is wrong or out of date.
  • Withdraw consent - change their mind and have you stop the use they agreed to.
  • Erase (GDPR) - the "right to be forgotten": ask you to delete their data in certain cases.
  • Port (GDPR) - get their data in a common format to take elsewhere; the GDPR also adds a right to object.

The shared spirit is the first four - Singapore's PDPA gives access, correction, and withdrawal, and informs people through notice. The GDPR layers on erasure, portability, and objection. For this on-ramp, hold the shape: find it, show it, fix it, stop it - and know the EU asks for a little more.

Exercise 1 of 2

Label your own data ★ 12 min · pen and paper

Take the five data types you listed in Session 1 and put the new vocabulary to work on them. This is where the words stop being abstract.

Write your five data types down the left of a page (email list, HR records, CCTV, website analytics, that WhatsApp group).

For each, name the controller (who decided to collect it - usually you) and the processor (the tool or vendor that handles it for you).

Ask: did we get consent or otherwise have a good reason, and did we state a purpose at the time? Mark yes, no, or not sure.

Any "no" or "not sure" is a governance to-do. You have just done your first data-and-vocabulary map.

Exercise 2 of 2

Three questions about rights ★ 8 min · your next team chat

If a customer emailed today and exercised a right, could you answer? These three questions tell you fast.

Find: "If one person asked what data we hold about them, could we pull it together - or is it scattered across five tools no one owns?"

Fix: "If they told us a detail is wrong, do we have a clear way to correct it everywhere it lives?"

Stop: "If they withdrew consent to marketing, could we actually stop - and would every tool respect that?"

The mindset Rights are not paperwork - they are the moment a person tests whether your promises were real. An organisation that can find, show, fix, and stop on request is one that has its data house in order. That readiness is the whole point of governance.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Your shop hires a cloud email tool to send newsletters you designed to a list you built. In that arrangement, who is the controller and who is the processor?

The controller decides the purpose and means - that is your shop. The processor only acts on the controller's instructions - that is the email tool (the PDPA would call it a "data intermediary").

2 · You collected emails to send order receipts, then start using them for marketing without asking again. Which promise did you break?

Consent is permission for a stated purpose; purpose limitation says stick to it. A new purpose (marketing) needs a fresh basis - usually asking again.

3 · Which right is one the GDPR adds that Singapore's PDPA does not give in the same way?

Access and correction are shared in spirit by both. Erasure ("right to be forgotten") and portability are the extras the GDPR layers on top.
