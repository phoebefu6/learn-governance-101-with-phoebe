Data & AI Governance, from scratch · Session 1 of 6

What is data, and why does anyone govern it?

Before the acronyms - PDPA, GDPR, the EU AI Act - there is one plain idea: your organisation holds information about real people, and how you treat it earns or loses their trust, creates or avoids risk, and follows or breaks the law. This first hour builds the ground floor: what "personal data" actually means, and the three reasons governance exists. No jargon, no statutes. Just the mental model everything else hangs on.

Part 0

Why start here, and not with the law

Most governance training opens with a wall of acronyms and section numbers, and loses the room in five minutes. We do the opposite. Governance is not, at heart, a legal topic - it is a trust topic that the law happens to enforce. If you understand what data is and why it matters to the people it describes, every rule that follows makes sense as a consequence, not a hoop. So this hour has exactly two jobs: define "personal data" so you can spot it anywhere, and give you the three reasons - trust, risk, law - that governance exists at all.

Part 1 · covers what personal data is

Data, and the slice of it that is about people 9 min live

"Data" is any recorded fact - a temperature reading, a sales total, a stock count. Governance mostly cares about a narrower slice: personal data, meaning information about an identified or identifiable living person. The test is simple: could this, alone or combined with other things you hold, point to one specific human being? If yes, it is personal data, and the rules are about it.

LiveThe one test for "is this personal data?"3 min▶

You do not need to memorise a list. You need one question: "Could this information, by itself or combined with other things we hold, single out one living person?" If the answer is yes, treat it as personal data.

  • Obvious ones: name, NRIC or passport number, email, phone, home address, photo, date of birth.
  • Less obvious, still personal: a cookie ID, an IP address, a device ID, a GPS trail, a "customer #4471" that you can trace back to a person.
  • Not personal (usually): a warehouse temperature, total monthly revenue, a count of visits with no way to tie it to anyone. Aggregate and truly anonymous data falls outside.
★ Try it now (any chat AI)I run a small online shop. Here is a list of the fields we store about customers: [paste your fields]. For each, tell me whether it is personal data - could it identify one living person alone or combined with the others - and explain why in one line. Educational only.
LiveSensitive data - the data that needs extra care3 min▶

Inside personal data sits an even more careful slice: sensitive personal data (the GDPR calls it "special category"). It is the information that could hurt someone most if mishandled - health and medical records, race or ethnicity, religion, sexual orientation, political views, biometric data like fingerprints and face templates, and in Singapore, things like NRIC numbers get special handling.

  • The rule of thumb: the more sensitive the data, the higher the bar for collecting, storing, and sharing it.
  • You do not need the legal categories yet - just the instinct that health, identity, and beliefs deserve more protection than a shipping address.
Real world

The gym that stored too much. A neighbourhood gym collected members' names and emails (fine) but also asked for full medical histories on paper and left the folder at the front desk. That is sensitive personal data, handled with no protection - exactly the combination that turns a small business into a headline. The fix was not complicated: collect only what you need, lock up what you keep.

Self-studyData vs information vs a "data subject"2 min read▶

Three words get used loosely and it is worth pinning them down, because you will meet them everywhere. Data is the raw recorded fact ("2026-07-21", "Aisha", "clicked twice"). Information is data with meaning attached (Aisha visited on 21 July and clicked twice). And the person the data is about has a name in every privacy law: the data subject - the human whose personal data it is. Governance exists to protect the data subject, so keep that person in mind: behind every row in your database is someone who would have opinions about how you treat it. We meet the full vocabulary - controller, processor, consent, purpose - in Session 2.

Part 2 · covers why governance exists

Three reasons anyone bothers: trust, risk, law 8 min live

If personal data is the "what", governance is the "how we look after it". And there are exactly three reasons an organisation does it. They stack: the first is why it is right, the second is why it is smart, the third is why it is not optional. Most leaders lead with the third - the law - but the first two are what make a program stick.

LiveTrust: the reason before the rules2 min▶

People hand over their data on an unspoken promise: use this for what I expect, keep it safe, and do not surprise me. Break that promise - sell it on, lose it, use it for something creepy - and the damage is not a fine, it is trust, which is far harder to win back. Governance is, first, keeping that promise. The organisations that get this treat privacy as part of the product, not a compliance tax.

LiveRisk: the reason the CFO cares3 min▶

Mishandled data is a business risk with a price tag. A breach means incident costs, notification duties, lost customers, and sometimes regulatory fines. Misusing data - even legally grey use - invites complaints and brand damage. Governance is how you make that risk small and predictable instead of large and sudden.

Real world

Meridian's good week that could have been bad. A mid-size retailer, Meridian, spotted that an old marketing list with 30,000 emails was sitting unencrypted on a shared drive. Because they had a simple governance habit - a quarterly "what data do we hold, and where?" review - they caught it and locked it down before anyone else found it. No headline, no fine. That quiet non-event is exactly what good governance looks like: the disaster that did not happen.

LiveLaw: the reason it is not optional3 min▶

Finally, the law makes parts of this mandatory, with teeth. You will meet three regimes at a glance in Session 3, so just hold the shape for now:

  • Singapore's PDPA - the baseline for handling personal data in Singapore, plus the Do Not Call rules for marketing.
  • The EU's GDPR - the world's most influential privacy law, and it can reach a Singapore company that serves people in the EU.
  • The EU AI Act - the first big law specifically for AI systems, risk-tiered, and it too can reach beyond Europe.

The fines are real (the GDPR's top tier runs into the tens of millions of euros), but notice the order: the law is reason three. If you only ever chase the law, you build a brittle checklist. If you lead with trust and risk, compliance tends to follow.

Governance in one sentence "Governance is how we make sure the data and AI we use is handled in a way we can trust, defend, and stand behind." Say that in your next meeting and you will be ahead of most rooms.
Exercise 1 of 2

Find the personal data around you ★ 12 min · pen and paper

Governance gets real the moment you point it at your own data. Do this for the organisation you know best.

List five things your organisation stores about people - customers, staff, or leads. Do not overthink it: email list, HR records, CCTV, the website's analytics, the WhatsApp group.

For each, apply the one test: could it single out one living person, alone or combined? Mark it personal / not personal.

Now flag any that are sensitive - health, identity numbers, anything that could really hurt if leaked. Those need the most care.

For the most sensitive one, ask: do we actually need it, and is it protected? If the answer is "not sure", you have found your first governance task.

Exercise 2 of 2

Say the "why" out loud ★ 8 min · your next team chat

Three questions that turn the trust/risk/law idea into something your team can act on.

Trust: "What would our customers be uncomfortable to learn we do with their data?" If you can name it, you have found a trust risk worth fixing.

Risk: "If we lost our biggest customer list tomorrow, what would it cost us - in money and in trust?" That number is your motivation.

Law: "Do we serve or affect anyone outside Singapore - in the EU especially?" Hold the answer; Session 3's chooser will tell you which laws that pulls in.

The mindset You do not need to be a lawyer to start governing data well. You need to know what personal data is, why it matters, and who to ask next. That is this course. The law is the floor; trust is the goal.

1 · Your analytics store a "customer #4471" that your team can trace back to a named person. Is it personal data?

The test is whether it can identify one living person alone or combined with what else you hold. A traceable ID, cookie, or IP all qualify.

2 · Which is the best one-line reason to govern data, if you want a program that lasts?

Trust and risk are why governance sticks; the law makes parts mandatory but a law-only checklist tends to be brittle.

3 · "Sensitive personal data" (special category) means...

Sensitive / special-category data (health, race, religion, sexual orientation, biometrics, and the like) carries a higher bar for how you collect, store, and share it.
